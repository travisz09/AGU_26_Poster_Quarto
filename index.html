1 Methods

1.1 Model Training and Validation

Machine learning (ML) classification of Landsat images was based on a modified version of methods published by Aberle et al. (2025). Initial training data (labeled points and images) was acquired courtesy of Aberle et al. for 4 USGS Benchmark glaciers (Gulkana, South Cascade, Sperry, and Wolverine) across 7 Landsat images, for a total of 8,162 training points. These labeled training points were augmented with an additional 2,650 training points within Greenland, for a combined total of 10,812 training points. For Greenland training data, two locations were chosen, Mittivakkat glacier in south-east Greenland, and a heavily glaciated rocky peninsula in west-central Greenland (72°N, 54°W; north of Disko Island). Additionally, the “cloud” class was added to the list of 5 landcover types from Aberle et al. (2025), bringing the total number of land cover classifications to 6, “cloud”, “ice/firn”, “rock”, “shadowed snow”, “snow”, and “water”.

Surface reflectance Landsat 8 & 9 images were acquired for each of these training locations/dates via Google Earth Engine (GEE). Where multiple Landsat images overlapped within a 1-hour window for a single glacier (i.e. successive Landsat rows), images were merged using a median pixel value, as described by Aberle et al. (2025). For each training image five spectral indices were calculated to aid in image classification; Normalized Difference Snow Index (NDSI), Normalized Difference Glacier Index (NDGI), Normalized Difference Water Index (NDWI), Normalized SWIR Difference Soil-moisture (NSDS), and Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) (Equations 1 to 5). Each of these indices was tested individually and in combination with the other bands/indices and was found to aid separation of one or more of the landcover classes of interest. Surface reflectance, surface temperature, and all 5 spectral indices data were extracted from pixel data at each of the labeled training data points (Figures 1 & 2).

\[ NDSI = \frac{green - SWIR_1}{green + SWIR_1} = \frac{SR\_B3 - SR\_B6}{SR\_B3 + SR\_B6} \tag{1}\] \[ NDGI = \frac{green - red}{green + red} = \frac{SR\_B3 - SR\_B4}{SR\_B3 + SR\_B4} \tag{2}\] \[ NDWI = \frac{green - NIR}{green + NIR} = \frac{SR\_B3 - SR\_B5}{SR\_B3 + SR\_B5} \tag{3}\] \[ NSDS = \frac{SWIR_1 - SWIR_2}{SWIR_1 + SWIR_2} = \frac{SR\_B6 - SR\_B7}{SR\_B6 + SR\_B7} \tag{4}\] \[ NDVI = \frac{NIR - red}{NIR + red} = \frac{SR\_B5 - SR\_B4}{SR\_B5 + SR\_B4} \tag{5}\]

For model training and testing a K-folds repeated cross-validation method was utilized with 10 folds, 10 repeats, and a partition (p) = 0.9 resulting in a 90/10 training/testing split, where all labeled data points are utilized in testing for exactly 1 of 10 folds/repeats. Using this training/testing framework a total of 129 distinct ML models were tested, across 10 model types, using a hyper-parameter grid search performed in R utilizing the caret package (Kuhn 2008). Based on the accuracy results of this training/testing model optimization process, the three best performing models were selected for additional model validation using previously unseen, spatially-temporally distant validation data. The top three, best performing models were, random forest, support vector machine (SVM) using a linear kernel, and SVM with a radial kernel Table 1.

Due to the spatial auto-correlation of the training/testing data, K-folds methods are prone to over-inflating model accuracy with geospatial datasets. Therefore, it is best practice to validate spatial ML models using an additional dataset which is spatially-temporally distant from the training/testing data and which has never been seen by the model. In our study, we labeled 5,750 Greenland-specific validation points for 5 locations, across 8 Landsat images. These locations were chosen for validation to capture a range of regional variability across the island. The five validation locations are, Mittivakkat glacier (3 dates/images), central-west peninsula (2), southern tip (1), Freya glacier (central-east, 1), and far north (1). (Validation point locator map figure).

The three best performing ML models were retrained using 100% of the available training data (10,812 labeled points) and were further validated on previously unseen data. The overall accuracy of all models decreased between 1-8% from their prior K-folds accuracy, but maintained a satisfactory ovrall accuracy. The best performing model on the validation data was the SVM linear model, with an overall accuracy of 93% (Figure 4). The F1 statistic (a measure of model performance) was 94% for snow and 91% for ice, the two most critical classes for accurate Accumulation Area Ratio (AAR) calculation.

1.2 Land Cover Classification

1.2.1 Data Acquisition

Following model validation, all applicable Landsat 8 & 9 Collection 2 Level-2, Tier 1 surface reflectance Science Products images from 2015-2025 during the ablation season (JJAS) which contained at least one peripheral glacier were downloaded using either GEE geedim python package or the United States Geological Survey (USGS) EarthExplorer data portal. Images taken on ascending orbits, and images which only covered the Greenland ice sheet but did not contain any peripheral glaciers were excluded. In total, 20,562 unique images were downloaded (~17 TB).

1.2.2 Image Preprocessing and Classification

Landsat images and the pretrained SVM model were uploaded to the Arizona State University (ASU) Sol super computer (Sol) for processing (Jennewein et al. 2023). Raw Landsat images were initially converted to surface reflectance and surface temperature data using established scaling factors for the OLI and TIRS instruments respectively. Images were then masked to peripheral glaciers using a buffered version of the Randolf Glacier Inventory (RGI) glacier outlines (RGI Consortium 2023). A secondary cloud mask was applied based on a modified version of the Antarctic cloud mask developed by Moussavi et al. (2020). After masking, the five indices were calculated as with the training dataset.

Preprocessed images were classified according to the pretrained SVM (linear) model. For each classified image, glaciers with at least 70% classification coverage were identified by a spatial query with the image extent. For each of the glaciers identified the surface area for each landcover class was extracted from the classified image along with relevant image metadata. For each glacier observation the transient AAR was calculated according to equations 6 to 8. Each glacier observation and associated calculations was appended to a data file, culminating in ~4.6 million glacier observations. This data file was downloaded for further post-processing.

\[ Total\:Snow\:Area\:(km^2) = snow + shadowed\:snow \tag{6}\] \[ Total\:Glacier\:Area\:(km^2) = Total\:Snow\:Area + ice/firn \tag{7}\] \[ Transient\:AAR = \frac{Total\:Snow\:Area}{Total\:Glacier\:Area} \tag{8}\]

1.3 Postprocessing

Glacier observation data was postprocessed in R using the data.table package for fast, efficient handling of large datasets (R Core Team 2021; Barrett et al. 2026). Spatial data was handled using the sf package (Pebesma 2018).

1.3.1 Quality Control and Data Filtering

A variety of quality control and filtering steps were utilizedTo ensure that appropriate annual (end-of-season) AAR values were identified from the transient AAR dataset. First, glacier observations were filtered by their calculated glacier area. Then they were filtered by cloud cover, shadowed snow ratio, glacier size, glacier slope, and finally by date.

The first observation filtering process leveraged the glacier area to identify and screen poorly classified images. The glacier area is calculated according to Equation 7, and the glacier area ratio is standardized by dividing the glacier area by its RGI glacier extent (circa 2000). Ideally, throughout the ablation season, the glacier area ratio is expected to vary smoothly from ~1 (spring; fully snow covered) to some minimum value (late summer; exposed ice and rock near terminous), followed by a rapid rebound (end of ablation; onset of seasonal snowfall). In practice a combination of cloudy images and poor image classification can drive the glacier area ratio down. To screen these poor quality images a loess function was fit to the glacier area ratio data for each glacier-year. The residuals were calculated for each glacier observation, and observations with residuals < the 25th percentile per glacier-year were filtered out (Figure 5).

Several additional simple filters were applied to the transient AAR data according to AAR accuracy spot-checks and sensitivity analysis Table 2. After all other filters were applied, the number of remaining quality glacier observations were counted for each glacier year. Glacier years which did not have at least 9 quality observations were removed.

1.3.2 Annual AAR determination

From this filtered transient AAR dataset, the annual (end-of-season) AAR was identified as the minimum remaining transient AAR value (hereafter, AAR is synonymous with annual AAR). All late season glacier observations occurring after the AAR date were filtered, and the number of remaining glacier observations during the abblation period were counted. If a glacier-year had at least 9 quality observations during the ablation period (Section 1.4), then the AAR was considered valid, and the data was saved to a separate intermediate datafile. In total 37,036 glacier-years had quality AAR data.

1.3.3 Mean AAR

The mean AAR (\(\overline{AAR}\)) was calculated as the arithmetic mean of AAR for glaciers which had at least 5 years of quality data. \(\overline{AAR}\) was saved to a separate datafile with 3,679 glaciers.

1.4 Glacier-Year Observation Density Sensitivity Analysis

To quantify the number of quality images needed for accurate identification of the AAR, an observational density experiment was performed. Twenty-one well-observed glacier-years were identified, evenly distributed around Greenland, each with at least 30 quality glacier observations (post filtering). For each glacier a series of subseting experiments was performed where n randomly selected points were subset from the glacier-year data. Using the randomly subset data, the assumed AAR was selected and compared against the actual (known) AAR from the full dataset. The n observations tested were; 3, 5, 7, 9, 12, 15, 20, 25, 30, and each experiment was repeated 100 times, for a total of 18,900 simulations Figure 6. Based on the resultus of these simulations, at least 9 quality observations per year are needed to reliably identify the AAR Table 3.

1.5 Equilibrium AAR and Committed Area Loss

Equilibrium AAR (AAR0) values were given by Yang et al. (2026). Disequalibrium (\(\alpha\)) and committed area loss (\(\Delta\)A) were calculated according to Box et al. (2022). The \(\Delta\)A values varried considerably by region, with the central east (CE), north east (NE), south west (SW) all showing positive committed area change (i.e., committed growth). Furthermore, while the resulting \(\Delta\)A values are approximately centered on 0 (median = -0.26 km2) the distribution is not normal (Figure 7). Rather, the distribution is highly leptokurtic, with a slight positive skew. This indicates that, while most glaciers are near their predicted equilibrium value, the distribution is highly dominated by outliers on either side.

1.5.1 Committed Area Loss Sensitivity Analysis

To further investigate the \(\Delta\)A results we conducted a sensitivity analysis for a range of plausible AAR0 values, from 0.5 to 0.8, for each region individually as well as for Greenland as a whole (Figure 8). While most regions exhibited relatively low sensitivity to AAR0 values, the NE and CE were both highly sensitive to a wide range of AAR0 values. Additionally, the SW was moderatly sensitive to AAR0 values between 0.5 and 0.6. Importantly, these are also the three regions which are indicating potential glacier growth. Therefore, small differences in either \(\overline{AAR}\) or AAR0 in any of these regions would result in relatively large changes to \(\Delta\)A. Additionally, while the CE continues to show positive \(\Delta\)A values up to AAR0 ≈ 0.68, the \(\Delta\)A for the NE crossess 0 at AAR0 ≈ 0.58 a difference of just 0.02 relative to the Yang et al. (2026) data.

1.5.2 Review of Yang et al. Methods and Accuracy within Greenland

While the Yang et al. (2026) data is laudible, and on of the only glacier-specific AAR0 datasets available, it may not be well suited for use in Greenland. For example, consider this large glacier in the north west (NW) (RGI2000-v7.0-G-05-00250; Figure 9). This glacier is the most extreme example of a positive \(\Delta\)A in the dataset. With an \(\overline{AAR}\) of 0.63 and a realized area loss of < 2% from circa 2020 to 2025 this glacier appears to be relatively healthy. However, the Yang et al. (2026) data lists the AAR0 for this glacier as 0.45, a shockingly low estimate. In fact, of the top 10 most positive \(\Delta\)A glaciers in our data, 7 of them have AAR0 values < 0.5 (the losest tested theoretical AAR0 value in our sensitivity analysis). With global paleio-climate AAR0 estimates typically approximated at 0.58, values below 0.5 are quite low. Additionally, ice caps typically have larger AAR0 values, relative to equivalent mountain glaciers.

These results call into question the accuracy of the Yang et al. (2026) data within Greenland. Despite these concerns, it is not clear if the Yang et al. (2026) is any more or less accurate than alternate AAR0 estimates, including global paleoclimate estimates. Therefore, we present \(\Delta\)A values preliminaraly.
